## THE CORE POLITICAL STORY (One-Page Summary)

### Income Reality
- **Firm-level wage loss** (8.5% for full sample, 17.6% for women, 15.8% for older workers)
- **Occupation-level wage gain** (10.9% from selection of high-wage firms into AI adoption)
- **Political implication**: Workers experience loss locally but see aggregate gains nationally—"my firm lost, but the occupation gained"

### Political Response
- **Primary shift**: Leftward on general political ideology (-0.25 points overall, -0.43 for men)
- **Secondary shifts**: Minimal effects on specific policies (welfare, redistribution, immigration)
- **Mechanism**: Response to perceived technological threat, not current income loss
- **Political implication**: Workers demand **state protection ideology**, not specific policies—gives parties room to propose alternatives

### Gender Heterogeneity
- **Women**: Strong economic threat (job insecurity +0.155) but ZERO political response (-0.012)
- **Men**: Weak economic threat (job insecurity +0.070) but STRONG political response (-0.430)
- **Political implication**: Economic threat ≠ automatic political mobilization; efficacy beliefs, occupational identity, household positioning all matter

### Age Heterogeneity
- **Older workers**: Strong leftward shift (-0.46) but MIXED on specific policies (rightward on immigration +0.26)
- **Young workers**: No political response (-0.14)
- **Political implication**: Older workers seek economic protection but resist progressive social policies; age stratifies vulnerability

### Policy Window
- **Timing**: Workers respond to anticipated threat BEFORE experiencing full economic loss
- **Opportunity**: Early policy intervention (retraining, regulation, wage insurance) can prevent political entrenchment
- **Risk**: If government delays, political demand solidifies and becomes harder to satisfy

## KEY CLAIMS (With Confidence Levels)

### High Confidence (p < 0.05, robust across specs)
- Male workers shift leftward on political spectrum in response to AI exposure
- Wage losses concentrate on women and older workers in AI-adopting firms
- Effect is larger in vulnerability groups (older, female)

### Medium Confidence (p = 0.05–0.10, directionally correct but borderline)

## WHAT THIS RESEARCH DOES AND DOESN'T PROVE

### DOES Prove
- **Association**: AI exposure correlates with leftward political shift
- **Heterogeneity**: Effect is 40x larger for men than women
- **Concentration**: Wage losses concentrate on vulnerable groups
- **Mechanism**: Job insecurity explains only 1-2% of shift; direct threat perception dominates
- **Timing**: Workers respond before experiencing full economic loss

### DOES NOT Prove
- **Causality**: Selection bias not ruled out (lead-lag test needed)
- **Generalizability**: Results apply to formal-sector employees with firm linkage (23% of SHP)
- **Policy preferences**: Direct observation of what workers want (inferred from ideology shift)
- **Long-term dynamics**: Entrenchment vs. adaptation pattern (need more years of data)
- **Policy effectiveness**: Whether government response actually reduces political demand

## FINAL THOUGHT

Your findings reveal a **politically mobilizable coalition of losers** from AI adoption: women in routine jobs, older workers, low-wage administrative staff. This coalition is **ideologically coherent** (demand for state protection) but **policy-preference opaque** (we don't know their specific policy wants). 

The political window is **open early**—workers demand intervention before they've experienced full economic loss. This creates an **opportunity for proactive policy**. But the window is **closing**: as AI adoption accelerates, political demand will solidify and become harder to satisfy.

**The next 12–18 months are critical** for establishing whether government can:
1. Credibly commit to worker protection policies
2. Prevent full political entrenchment
3. Maintain productivity gains while protecting vulnerable workers

These documents give you the roadmap. The question is whether policy responds.
