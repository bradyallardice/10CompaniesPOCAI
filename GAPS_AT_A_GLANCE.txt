INCOME REVERSAL ANALYSIS: FIVE GAPS AT A GLANCE
================================================================================

YOUR FINDING:
  AI exposure → -8.5% wage loss (FY-FE specification)
  Heterogeneity: Women -17.6%, Older workers -15.8%

THE CORE PROBLEM:
  This is a valid LABOR MARKET finding, but not yet a POLITICAL ECONOMY finding.
  Gap: From "workers lose wages" to "workers demand policy change due to AI"

================================================================================
GAP 1: MECHANISM AMBIGUITY
================================================================================

You know:         Workers exposed to AI lose wages
You don't know:   WHY does wage loss occur?

Possible mechanisms (all consistent with same coefficient):

  A. Task Displacement
     AI automates worker's tasks → reassigned to lower-value work
     → Same job title, lower wage

  B. Occupational Downgrading
     Worker demoted to lower-wage role within firm
     → New job title, lower wage

  C. Bargaining Power Erosion
     Worker loses wage negotiations because AI makes them substitutable
     → Same job title, same tasks, lower wage

  D. Hours Reduction
     Worker retains position but works fewer hours
     → Same hourly wage, fewer hours total

  E. Selection on Unobservables
     Firms assign low-ability workers to AI roles
     → Wage loss reflects ability, not AI treatment

Political implications DIFFER COMPLETELY:
  A → Demand retraining, education, tech regulation
  B → Demand occupational protections, firm accountability
  C → Demand unions, worker power strengthening
  D → Demand labor standards, hour guarantees
  E → No real grievance (outcome reflects ability)

HOW TO TEST:
  Track job titles pre/post AI adoption within firms
  Decompose wage loss into within-title (bargaining) vs. between-title (downgrade)

STATUS: CRITICAL — Different mechanisms → different political demands

================================================================================
GAP 2: SELECTION ON UNOBSERVABLES
================================================================================

Your spec:        Firm-Year FE (controls for firm-level heterogeneity)
Your assumption:  Workers randomly assigned to AI exposure (given firm, year)
Reality check:    Firms might preferentially assign low-ability workers to AI roles

Example scenario:
  Firm: "Data entry is routine, hire less-experienced staff"
  Later: "Automate data entry, reassign workers to support"
  You observe: Wage loss in assigned cohort
  But true cause: Worker quality, not AI treatment

Problem: Both situations produce same FY-FE coefficient (-0.085)

HOW TO TEST:
  1. Plot wage trends for future-treated workers 3 years BEFORE AI adoption
     - If causal: trends should be parallel to untreated
     - If selection: trends already diverging before treatment

  2. Placebo test: Assign fake exposure 1-2 years BEFORE actual adoption
     - If causal: no wage loss in fake pre-treatment period
     - If selection: wage loss already appears in pre-treatment

  3. Altonji bounds: Quantify robustness to unobserved selection
     - Report: "Effect robust if unobserved selection < X times observed"

TIMELINE: 3-5 days per test
STATUS: THREATENS ENTIRE ANALYSIS — If selection explains effect, all downstream findings unreliable

================================================================================
GAP 3: EXPOSURE MEASUREMENT IS COARSE
================================================================================

Your measure:     AI exposure from job ads at firm-occupation-year level
Reality:          Job ads are a proxy for actual task displacement

Problems:
  • Time lag: Firms post AI jobs AFTER internal adoption (1-2 year lag)
  • Measure: Ads show demand for AI skills, not displacement of existing tasks
  • Granularity: Only occupation level, not individual tasks
  • Sparsity: Only 6,139/45,325 (13.5%) person-years have exposure > 0
               Median exposure = 0.0000, 75th pct = 0.0000
  • Selection: High-status firms advertise AI heavily; low-status firms adopt quietly

You're comparing:  "Firm posted AI ads" vs. "didn't"
You're not comparing: "Worker's actual tasks changed" vs. "didn't"

HOW TO TEST:
  1. Link job ads to individual respondents by role (not just occupation)
  2. Vary lag structure (t, t+1, t+2) and test effect persistence
  3. Control for firm posting volume (large firms post more ads)

TIMELINE: 1-2 weeks
STATUS: HIGH — Measurement error is systematic, not random

GAP 4: INCOMPLETE HETEROGENEOUS EFFECTS
================================================================================

You have:         Gender (women -17.6%, men -1.7%)
                  Age (older -15.8%, young +2.3%)

You need:         Education × Sector × Firm Size matrix

Why it matters:   Different combinations have different:
  • Retraining capacity (college-educated can retrain; HS cannot)
  • Task structure (manufacturing routine automation ≠ finance decision support)
  • Firm dynamics (SMEs can't retrain; large firms have internal mobility)
  • Political mobilization potential (visible, concentrated losses mobilize more)

Example vulnerability patterns you should identify:
  High-risk cells: HS education × Manufacturing × SME (large loss, many workers)
  Protected cells: Bachelor+ × Finance × Large firm (small/no loss)

HOW TO TEST:
  Create heatmap: β(AI exposure) for each education × sector × size cell
  Highlight cells with: (|β| > 0.10, n > 100, mean_exposure > 0.05)
  These are politically consequential vulnerability clusters

TIMELINE: 1-2 weeks
STATUS: MEDIUM — Needed for political targeting, not for validating wage effect

GAP 5: SAMPLE SELECTION BIAS
================================================================================

Your sample:      37,279 person-years from SHP with exposure and income data
Population:       SHP includes ~345,000 person-year observations

Missing:          ~75% of SHP respondents have no firm_id (structural missing)
                  Only 8-13% per year are linked to firms

Who's missing?    Unknown—possibly:
                  • Self-employed (no firm ID system)
                  • Small-firm employees (hard to link)
                  • Contract/temp workers (don't fit firm model)
                  • Recent immigrants (linkage doesn't work)

Likely bias:      Sample over-represents:
                  • Large firms (more linkable, more data)
                  • Structured employment (easier to match)
                  • Higher-wage sectors (more administrative record-keeping)

Consequence:      Your wage loss estimate applies to large-firm workers
                  May not represent small-firm workers (more vulnerable)
                  Generalizability to full Swiss labor force unclear

HOW TO TEST:
  1. Compare your sample to full SHP: demographics, sector, income distribution
  2. Compare to Swiss census/LFS: national representative benchmark
  3. Check if exposure correlates with firm size in your sample
  4. Use inverse-probability weighting (IPW) to adjust for selection

TIMELINE: 1-2 weeks
STATUS: MEDIUM — Affects generalizability, not internal validity of your estimate
