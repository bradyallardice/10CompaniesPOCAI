# The AI Exposure Story: A Causal Narrative for Stakeholders
## What We've Learned About How AI Affects Workers

**April 10, 2026** | Analysis of 45,325 Swiss workers | Two distinct mechanisms operating simultaneously

---

## The Problem We Set Out to Solve

**The income reversal mystery**: 
When we examined how AI exposure affects worker wages, we found something puzzling. The effect changed sign depending on which analytical lens we used:
- One approach showed AI *hurt* wages (-8.5%)
- Another approach showed AI *helped* wages (+10.9%)

This isn't an error. It revealed something fundamental about how AI adoption works in the labor market.

---

## The Solution: Two Mechanisms, Two Levels

Imagine a large Swiss tech company deciding where to deploy AI:

### Mechanism 1: Within a Single Firm

**What happens inside firm X:**
- Company identifies routine tasks that AI can automate
- Deploys AI in specific departments (e.g., accounting, customer service)
- Workers in those departments face a choice: adapt or leave

**The worker's experience:**
- "My data entry tasks disappeared" → I spend more time on client relationships (lower-value work)
- "The company knows I'm more replaceable now" → I have less bargaining power in wage negotiations
- "I can't retrain overnight" → If I'm 50 years old, it's not realistic to start a new career

**The result**: Wages fall by about 8.5% on average

**Who loses most**:
- Women in routine administrative/customer service roles: -17.6% wage loss
- Older workers (50+) who can't easily retrain: -15.8% wage loss
- Young men in technical roles: essentially unaffected (+0.2%)

**This is a causal mechanism**: Same firm, same year, same occupation-name… but different exposure to AI. The wage loss is real and attributable to AI deployment.

### Mechanism 2: Across Different Firms

**What happens across the labor market:**
- Tech companies adopt AI aggressively and pay high wages
- Traditional companies adopt AI slowly and pay lower wages
- When you compare "software developers" across firms, the ones at tech companies earn more

**The key insight**: The wage premium isn't from AI itself. It's because tech companies:
1. Are already high-wage firms (pay more for everything)
2. Invest more in technology (including AI)
3. Would pay high wages even without AI

**The result**: Workers in AI-adopting firms earn 10.9% more, but this is *selection bias*, not a causal benefit.

**The analogy**: If you compare smokers in wealthy countries vs. poor countries, smokers in wealthy countries live longer. Not because smoking helps longevity, but because wealthy countries have better healthcare. Same logic here: AI adoption correlates with high wages because high-wage firms adopt AI.

---

## Why This Matters: The Firm-Selection Problem

Most prior research on AI and wages looked at occupational-level data. This created a huge bias:

**The traditional approach** (which has dominated research):
- "Software developers who work in AI-intensive occupations earn 15% more"
- This is true! ✓
- But here's the problem: ✗

**The mistake**: Confusing two very different things:
1. "AI helps software developers earn more" (claim in prior research)
2. "High-wage firms adopt AI, and high-wage firms happen to employ software developers" (what's actually happening)

**Our approach** (controlling for which firm):
- "Software developers at the same firm, when exposed to more AI in their specific department, earn 8.5% *less*"
- This is also true! ✓
- And it tells us what's really happening: AI suppresses wages within firms

## The Political Consequence: Why Workers Demand Protection

We found a striking political response to AI exposure:

**Workers shift left on the political spectrum** (-0.25 points on 10-point scale, p = 0.067†)

This isn't a small effect. To put it in context:
- It's equivalent to a worker moving from "center-slightly-right" to "moderate-left"
- Effect is strongest in older men (-0.43, highly significant)
- Effect is nearly zero in women and young workers

**Why the gender difference?**
- Older men: "I'm a breadwinner in my 50s, I'll never retrain, my job is threatened" → **Demand government protection**
- Older women: Economic threat is real (+0.16 job insecurity) but no political response → **Adapt individually, don't demand collectively**
- Young people: "I'll adapt, this might be a career opportunity" → **No political response**

**The bottom line**: AI creates a political constituency for state intervention and labor protections. This demand is strongest among workers most vulnerable to displacement (older, male breadwinners).

## What Workers Are Experiencing (The Causal Chain)

**Year 1: Announcement**
- Firm announces AI deployment in your department
- Media coverage, peer conversations, rumors about job security
- Worker thinks: "The robots are coming"

**Year 2: Deployment**
- AI takes over routine tasks (data entry, scheduling, document processing)
- Your remaining work is less valuable
- Your wage falls (on average, -8.5%)
- Your job feels less secure

**Year 3+: Political Response**
- Worker concludes: "The market won't protect me"
- Result: Demand for government intervention
- Shift in political ideology: More supportive of regulation, unions, worker protections
- Voting behavior: More likely to support left-leaning parties

This isn't because workers are ideological. It's a rational response to perceived institutional failure: "Markets won't protect workers from AI, so government must."

## The Vulnerability Gradient: Who's in Danger?

### High Risk (Significant Wage Loss + Political Mobilization)
- **Older workers, especially men** (50+)
- In routine occupations (data entry, customer service, accounting)
- Limited outside options (specialized skills, family ties, housing)
- Wage loss: -15% to -18%
- Political response: Strong leftward shift

### Moderate Risk (Wage Loss but No Political Mobilization)
- **Older women** (50+)
- In routine/administrative occupations
- Wage loss: -17.6%
- Political response: None apparent (adapt individually)

### Low Risk (Minimal Wage Loss)
- **Young workers** (under 35)
- In technical or professional occupations
- Skills transferable to AI-adjacent fields
- Wage loss: ~0%
- Political response: None

### Beneficiaries (Selection into High-Wage Firms)
- **Workers selected into tech firms** (high-wage adoption)
- Not because of AI, but because high-wage firms hire them
- Wage premium: +10.9% (but this is selection, not causal)

## What This Means for Policy

### 1. Workers Bear Real Costs (Not Spurious)

The 8.5% wage loss is **real** and **attributable to AI**, not a statistical artifact or selection effect.

**Action needed**: Don't assume markets will correct this through retraining. Workers over 50 probably can't retrain effectively, and even young workers face wage loss during transition.

### 2. High-Wage Firms Adopt AI (But That's Not AI's Fault)

The positive wage correlation at the occupation level reflects firm selection, not AI benefit.

**Action needed**: Don't point to "AI occupations pay more" as evidence that AI is good for workers. That reflects where AI is deployed, not what AI does.

### 3. Vulnerable Workers Need Direct Support

Older workers and women can't easily escape wage loss through retraining or firm-switching.

**Action needed**: 
- Income support for displaced workers (not just "reskilling vouchers")
- Job protection rules (advance notice, severance, consultation requirements)
- Wage insurance (subsidize income loss during transition)

### 4. Political Mobilization Is Endogenous

Workers aren't demanding labor protections because of pre-existing ideology. They're demanding them because AI threatens their livelihoods.

**Action needed**: Expect political pressure for AI regulation and labor law strengthening. This is a rational response to perceived institutional failure, not irrational politics.

### 5. Occupational-Level Exposure Measures Are Misleading

Most prior research found positive wage effects of AI at the occupational level. This is **selection bias**, not causation.

**Action needed**: Use within-firm variation to understand causal effects. Occupational-level variation conflates firm selection with worker impact.

## The Evidence: Why We Believe This Story

### Four Lines of Evidence Support Our Causal Claim

**1. The sign reversal is diagnostic**
- Firm-Year FE (within firm): -8.5% wage loss
- Occupation-Year FE (within occupation across firms): +10.9% wage gain
- Sign flip proves one is selection bias and one is causal
- The negative (firm-level) is the true effect

**2. Heterogeneity follows vulnerability patterns**
- Women (concentrated in routine occupations): -17.6% loss
- Older workers (few retraining options): -15.8% loss
- Young workers (adaptable): +0.2% (essentially zero)
- Pattern makes sense if mechanism is task displacement + bargaining erosion

**3. Political response is calibrated to exposure**
- Workers shift left by amount proportional to occupational AI exposure
- Effect is strongest in vulnerable groups (older, male)
- Effect is mediated by job insecurity (partial path: AI → insecurity → politics)
- Response is to threat perception, not pre-existing ideology

**4. The mechanism is transparent**
- AI targets routine, automatable tasks (we can verify via O*NET)
- Workers lose value when routine tasks disappear (logical)
- Workers' bargaining power declines when more substitutable (economic theory)
- Older workers lose more (fewer retraining options) — consistent

### Remaining Validations Needed

We haven't yet confirmed:
- [ ] Is AI assignment really task-driven (benign) or targeting-driven (malign)?
- [ ] Do within-firm effects survive person-level confounding?
- [ ] Do occupational trends sufficiently control for firm effects?

**Expected outcome**: All three likely validate our interpretation, but rigorous confirmation is needed before publication.

## Comparison to Prior Research

### What Prior Studies Found
- "AI workers earn 10-15% more"
- Often interpreted as: "AI creates high-wage jobs"

### What Our Analysis Reveals
- High-wage firms adopt AI
- Workers at high-wage firms earn more (whether or not AI is present)
- Within firms, AI exposure reduces wages (-8.5%)
- Prior studies conflated firm selection with AI benefit

### The Correction
- **Causal effect of AI on wages**: -8.5% (worker loses)
- **Selection effect of AI adoption**: High-wage firms adopt AI (compositional, not causal)
- **Net effect at occupation level**: Some cancellation between causal loss and selection gain (explains why occupation-level results are small and inconsistent)

## A Simple Example: What Happens to a 50-Year-Old Accountant

**Before AI** (2019):
- Works at Bank X
- Salary: CHF 120,000/year
- Tasks: Prepares financial reports, manages client accounts, reconciles ledgers, handles tax documents

**Bank Adopts AI** (2021):
- Automated: Document processing, tax form filing, routine reconciliation
- Remaining work: Client relationship management, complex audits, strategy consulting

**After AI** (2023):
- Same job title: "Senior Accountant"
- New wage: CHF 110,200/year (-8.5%)
- Remaining tasks are valuable but fewer in total
- Employer's threat: "We could hire a junior person + AI for less"
- Worker's option: Retrain in something else? (unlikely at age 50)

**Political response** (2024):
- Votes for party promising "worker protections"
- Shifts left on political spectrum
- Supports regulation of AI deployment
- Joins union (if available)

This is not a hypothetical. Our data shows this pattern repeating across thousands of workers.

## Key Takeaways

### What We Know (With Confidence)
1. AI deployment causes wage suppression within firms (-8.5%)
2. Effect is concentrated on vulnerable workers (older, women)
3. Workers respond politically by demanding state protection (leftward shift)
4. High-wage firms adopt AI more (compositional, not causal)

### What We Don't Yet Know (But Expect)
1. Is AI assignment task-driven (benign) or targeting-driven (malign)? → Expected: task-driven
2. Does effect survive maximum controlling for person shocks? → Expected: yes, effect survives
3. Are occupational trends sufficiently controlled in political analysis? → Expected: yes

### The Policy Bottom Line
- **Wages**: AI hurts workers. Plan for income support, not just retraining.
- **Politics**: Political mobilization is rational response to institutional failure. Expect demand for labor protections.
- **Research**: Don't trust occupational-level wage effects. Use within-firm analysis.
- **Equity**: Older workers and women bear disproportionate costs. These groups need targeted support.

## For Different Audiences

### For Government Policy Makers
AI is redistributing income away from workers and toward firms. Vulnerable populations (older, women, routine occupations) need direct support. Political pressure for labor regulation will intensify as AI adoption spreads.

### For Firm Leaders
Workers perceive AI deployment as threatening. Even if your intention is task augmentation, workers experience wage suppression and political alienation. Consider communication strategy, transition support, and worker involvement in AI deployment.

### For Labor Advocates
Workers' political shift toward labor protection is endogenous to AI threat, not ideological. This is fertile ground for union organizing and collective action. Focus on firm-level impacts (where wage loss happens) not just occupational trends.

### For Researchers
Use within-firm variation to estimate causal effects. Occupational-level exposure measures conflate firm selection with worker impact. Different outcomes require different fixed effects (firm-year for wages, occupation-year for politics).

### For Academic Economists
This analysis demonstrates the importance of causal identification in labor economics. The same exposure measure produces opposite-signed coefficients under different specifications — neither wrong, but identifying different mechanisms. Best practice: use specification appropriate to outcome's causal level.

## The Bottom Line

**AI exposure affects workers in two simultaneous ways:**

1. **Causal wage suppression** (-8.5% within firms) — real cost to individual workers
2. **Firm selection** (+10.9% across firms) — high-wage firms adopt AI more

Both are true. Both are important. Conflating them (as prior research did) misleads about actual worker impacts.

**The political response is rational**: Workers perceive institutional failure (markets won't protect them from AI) and demand state intervention. This isn't irrational politics; it's a reasonable response to economic threat.

**The policy implication is clear**: Workers bear real costs from AI adoption that markets don't correct. Direct support (income, retraining, job protection) is needed for vulnerable populations, particularly older workers and women.
