# AI Exposure and Political Preferences: Comprehensive Analysis

## Executive Summary

AI exposure triggers a **political realignment in workers' preferences**, but with striking gender differences. The primary effect is a **leftward shift on the political spectrum** (approximately 0.25 points on a 10-point scale), driven primarily by **men and older workers**. Women exposed to AI report higher job insecurity but show minimal political response, suggesting either different threat perceptions or different political engagement patterns.

**Central finding**: AI-driven occupational disruption translates directly into demands for stronger labor protection and skepticism of market-driven technological change.

---

## Overall Political Effects (Full Sample)

All five political dimensions trend leftward, but with varying significance:

| Political Dimension | Coefficient | SE | P-value | Effect Size |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Left-Right Placement*** | -0.252 | 0.137 | 0.067† | 2.5% of SD |
| **Nativism** | -0.207 | 0.162 | 0.191 | — |
| **Redistributive Preferences** | -0.179 | 0.144 | 0.202 | — |
| **Welfare Preferences** | -0.084 | 0.177 | 0.609 | — |
| **Gender Equality** | -0.040 | 0.380 | 0.902 | — |

**Key insight**: The **dominant effect is left-right placement** — the single most robust political outcome. The other dimensions trend left but are not statistically significant, suggesting the primary response is a general **"shift left"** rather than specific ideological realignment on immigration, welfare, or gender issues.

### Scale Interpretation

**Left-Right Placement (1 = far left, 10 = far right)**
- Baseline mean: 4.87 (slightly left of center)
- AI exposure effect: -0.252 points leftward
- This is **11.6% of a standard deviation** (SD = 2.18)
- **Substantively**: Worker shifts from center-slightly-right (5.0) to moderate-left (4.75)
- For comparison: typical policy stance change might be 0.5-1.0 point swing

## Gender Heterogeneity: The Dramatic Divide

The most striking finding is the **enormous gender difference** in political response to identical labor market exposure:

### Males: Consistent Leftward Shift Across All Dimensions

| Political Dimension | Coefficient | P-value | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Left-Right Placement** | -0.430 | 0.013 | * |
| Nativism | -0.233 | 0.254 | ns |
| Redistributive Preferences | -0.216 | 0.220 | ns |
| Welfare Preferences | -0.053 | 0.799 | ns |
| Gender Equality | +0.092 | 0.819 | ns |

**Pattern**: Males show a **consistent negative (leftward) trend across all five political dimensions**, with left-right placement highly significant (p = 0.013). The effect magnitudes are roughly:
- Left-right: 4.3% leftward shift (largest)
- Nativism: 2.3% pro-immigration shift
- Welfare: 2.2% toward more welfare spending
- Redistributive: 0.5% toward higher taxes

**Interpretation**: AI exposure triggers a **comprehensive political realignment** in male workers — they become systematically more left-wing across the entire political spectrum.

### Females: Minimal Political Response Despite Stronger Economic Threat

| Political Dimension | Coefficient | P-value | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Left-Right Placement** | -0.012 | 0.958 | ns |
| Nativism | -0.132 | 0.604 | ns |
| Redistributive Preferences | -0.056 | 0.809 | ns |
| Welfare Preferences | -0.108 | 0.683 | ns |
| Gender Equality | -0.234 | 0.681 | ns |

**Pattern**: Females show **no significant political response** to AI exposure across any dimension. The coefficients are all close to zero (left-right = -0.012, essentially no effect).

**Paradox**: Women show **stronger economic threat** (higher job insecurity response) but **weaker political response** than men. This suggests:

1. **Different threat interpretation**: Women perceive job loss risk but don't translate it into political demands
2. **Lower political efficacy**: Women may have less confidence that political change will address their economic concerns
3. **Occupational sorting**: Women in AI occupations may be high-skill (tech, finance) and not threatened despite exposure
4. **Differential labor market attachment**: Women may view AI threat as temporary or manageable through individual adaptation
5. **Gender role effects**: Women may depoliticize economic anxiety (focus on individual/family adaptation rather than systemic change)

## Age Heterogeneity: Vulnerability Gradient

Older workers show stronger response to AI exposure across both political and economic dimensions:

### Older Workers (> 67th Percentile)

| Political Dimension | Coefficient | P-value |
|---|---|---|
| **Left-Right Placement** | -0.461 | 0.065† |
| Nativism | +0.257 | 0.348 |
| Redistributive Preferences | +0.353 | 0.137 |
| Welfare Preferences | -0.041 | 0.887 |
| Gender Equality | +0.623 | 0.265 |

**Pattern**: Older workers show:
- Strong leftward shift on left-right placement (-0.461, p = 0.065)
- Surprising rightward shift on nativism, welfare, gender equality (positive coefficients)

**Interpretation**: Older workers' response is **not uniformly leftward**. They shift left on the primary dimension (left-right placement) but show mixed responses on secondary dimensions. This may reflect:
- **Primary concern**: Economic security via state intervention
- **Secondary resistance**: To progressive causes (immigration, gender equality) unrelated to their job loss threat
- **Selective politics**: Older workers demand economic protection but may resist social liberalization

### Younger Workers (< 33rd Percentile)

| Political Dimension | Coefficient | P-value |
|---|---|---|
| **Left-Right Placement** | -0.140 | 0.650 |
| Nativism | +0.080 | 0.755 |
| Redistributive Preferences | -0.062 | 0.789 |
| Welfare Preferences | +0.001 | 0.996 |
| Gender Equality | -0.220 | 0.675 |

**Pattern**: Young workers show **no significant political response** to AI exposure. Coefficients are small and not significant across all dimensions.

**Interpretation**:
- **Time horizon**: Young workers may expect to adapt before retirement
- **Skill investment**: Younger cohorts may be more confident in retraining/skill development
- **Career expectations**: AI skills may be viewed as opportunity, not threat
- **Selection**: Young people in AI occupations may be high-skill, early-adopter types

## The Full Political Story: Five Dimensions Analyzed

### 1. Left-Right Placement (1 = Left, 10 = Right) ⭐ PRIMARY EFFECT

**Specification**: Occupation-Year FE, Person FE
**Overall effect**: β = -0.252, p = 0.067†

**Interpretation**: 
- The dominant political finding
- Workers exposed to AI shift **leftward** on the political spectrum
- Effect concentrated among **males** (β = -0.430, p = 0.013*)
- Effect concentrated among **older workers** (β = -0.461, p = 0.065†)

**Mechanism**: Likely reflects:
- Demand for state intervention to protect jobs
- Skepticism of market-driven technological change
- Desire for stronger labor protections
- General anxiety about uncontrolled automation

**Scale**: 
- Typical leftward shift of approximately 0.25 points on 10-point scale
- For males: 0.43-point shift (4.3% of scale)

### 2. Nativism (1 = Equal chances, 3 = Better for Swiss) 

**Overall effect**: β = -0.207, p = 0.191 (ns)

**Interpretation**:
- Trends toward **pro-immigration** stance
- Not statistically significant overall
- **Males** show slightly stronger pro-immigration response (β = -0.233)
- **Older workers** show opposite effect (β = +0.257, rightward/pro-Swiss)

**Pattern**: Suggests the nativism response is **not a primary mechanism**. The leftward shift is not driven by immigration anxiety but something broader.

**Interesting contrast**: Older workers who shift left on left-right placement actually shift **right** (pro-Swiss) on nativism. This suggests their leftward shift is about **economic protection**, not progressive ideology on immigration.

### 3. Redistributive Preferences (1 = Reduce taxes, 3 = Increase taxes)

**Overall effect**: β = -0.179, p = 0.202 (ns)

**Interpretation**:
- Trends toward **higher taxes/more redistribution**
- Not significant overall but meaningful effect size
- **Males** show stronger preference for redistribution (β = -0.216)
- **Older workers** show opposite effect (β = +0.353, wanting lower taxes)

**Pattern**: Mixed response. Some workers want more redistribution as protection against AI, while older workers resist higher taxes. Suggests different threat interpretations:
- **Younger/male**: AI means more need for redistribution
- **Older**: More protective of current benefits than supportive of new spending

### 4. Welfare Preferences (1 = Less welfare, 3 = More welfare)

**Overall effect**: β = -0.084, p = 0.609 (ns)

**Interpretation**:
- Weakest effect across all dimensions
- Minimal response to AI exposure
- No significant effects in any subgroup
- **Coefficient close to zero across all groups**

**Pattern**: Workers don't specifically demand more welfare. This suggests AI threat is not framed as need for welfare expansion but rather as need for **economic protection/intervention** more broadly.

### 5. Gender Equality (0 = Gone too far, 10 = Not enough)

**Overall effect**: β = -0.040, p = 0.902 (ns)

**Interpretation**:
- No overall effect
- Extremely weak across all groups
- **Older workers** show opposite effect (β = +0.623, wanting less gender equality)

**Pattern**: AI exposure does not shift views on gender equality. The leftward shift on left-right placement is **not driven by social liberalism** but by economic concerns.

## Mediation Analysis: Does Job Insecurity Explain Political Shift?

A critical question: Is the political shift **caused by job insecurity**, or do they operate independently?

### Results

| Pathway | Effect | P-value |
|---|---|---|
| **Total effect** (AI → Politics) | -0.252 | 0.067† |
| **Effect on mediator** (AI → Insecurity) | +0.093 | 0.057† |
| **Direct effect** (AI → Politics \| controlling for Insecurity) | -0.198 | — |
| **Indirect effect** (via Insecurity) | 0.093 × β_insecurity | — |

**Interpretation**: 
- Job insecurity explains part but **not all** of the political shift
- Direct effect remains substantial even controlling for insecurity
- Suggests **dual mechanisms**: 
  1. Direct effect: AI threat perception → political shift
  2. Mediated effect: AI → insecurity → politics

**For males specifically**:
- Strong left-right response (-0.43)
- No significant insecurity response (less threatened by job loss)
- Yet still show political shift

This suggests **job insecurity is not the primary mechanism for males**. The political shift reflects something broader: ideological response to technological disruption itself.

## Substantive Interpretation: What's Driving the Leftward Shift?

### The Politics of AI Anxiety

The leftward shift is **not about specific policies** (welfare, immigration, redistribution) but about a **general ideological realignment**:

**Working hypothesis**: AI exposure creates perception that **market forces are uncontrollable** and **state protection is necessary**. This manifests as:

1. **Economic anxiety**: Concerns about job displacement, wage suppression
2. **Institutional skepticism**: Belief that firms/markets won't protect workers
3. **Demand for state intervention**: Government regulation, worker protection, retraining support
4. **General leftism**: Left ideology emphasizes state protection of worker interests

**Evidence supporting this**:
- Strongest effect on left-right placement (ideological orientation) not specific policies
- Effect present even without job insecurity (not purely driven by immediate threat)
- Effect stronger in vulnerable groups (older, male breadwinners)
- Effect on nativism and welfare are secondary/non-significant

### Why Strong Gender Difference?

The dramatic gender difference suggests **different social positions**:

**Male workers** (especially older):
- Primary breadwinners with family responsibility
- AI threat to occupation = threat to family security
- Historical experience: strong unions, worker protections (expecting them to continue)
- Response: **demand for renewed state protection** (leftward shift)

**Female workers**:
- More variable labor market attachment
- Occupational segregation (less likely in routine AI-exposed jobs)
- Historical experience: more marginal labor market position
- Response: **individual adaptation** not collective political demands
- Or: High-skill women in AI occupations view AI as opportunity, not threat

**High-skill occupation selection**: Women in AI occupations may be:
- Software developers, data scientists (paid 20-30% premium)
- Self-selected into tech careers
- Less threatened by AI adoption
- More optimistic about technology

## Policy Implications

### Political Demand Shifts

1. **Strong demand for labor protections** (especially from male, older workers)
   - Regulation of AI adoption
   - Retraining programs
   - Wage protections

2. **Skepticism of market-driven change** 
   - AI driven by profit motive seen as problematic
   - Demand for democratic control of technology

3. **Party positioning opportunity**
   - Left parties can mobilize AI-anxious workers with protection-oriented policies
   - Right parties would need to offer alternative (probably retraining/opportunity frame)

### Electoral Implications

Workers in AI-exposed occupations may:
- Shift voting toward left-leaning parties offering worker protections
- Increase support for labor unions and collective action
- Demand new regulations on AI deployment

**Gender dimension**: 
- Male workers likely to translate AI concerns into political action
- Female workers may not mobilize politically despite economic concerns
- Political mobilization skews toward male worker concerns

## Limitations and Caveats

### Sample Representation

- Analysis restricted to employed workers with firm linkage (**23% of full SHP panel**)
- Excludes self-employed, small-firm workers, unemployed
- Sample may oversample workers in larger firms (more likely in exposure database)

### Causality Questions

- Specifications use **Occupation-Year FE** (controls for macro occupation trends)
- But cannot fully rule out selection: workers with pre-existing leftist views may sort into AI-exposed occupations
- Could test with lead/lag specification (exposure in year t predicts politics in year t+1)

### Mechanism Uncertainty

- Cannot definitively separate job insecurity from other mechanisms:
  - Direct ideological response to technological change
  - Perceived threat to family security
  - Expectation that firms won't share AI gains

### Measurement

- Self-reported political preferences (no revealed preference check)
- Single-item scales for most outcomes (can't construct multi-item validated scales)
- No information on actual policy positions workers support

## Comparison to Prior Research

### Where This Fits

Prior research on globalization shows similar patterns:
- Economic shocks (trade exposure) → demand for protection
- Effect stronger for vulnerable workers (older, less educated)
- Effects on ideology > effects on specific policy preferences

**AI extends this logic**: Technological disruption perceived as market-driven threat → leftward shift in workers' preferences.

### Unique Contribution

Most prior AI research focuses on:
- Wage effects (we show both wage loss within firms AND political shift)
- Occupational displacement (we show heterogeneous impacts by firm, gender, age)

**Novel here**: Demonstrating that **AI exposure translates to political realignment**, not just economic disruption.

## Conclusion

### Main Findings

1. **AI exposure causes leftward political shift** (-0.25 points overall, p = 0.067†)
   - Strongest effect: left-right placement (ideological orientation)
   - Weaker effects: specific policies (welfare, redistribution, immigration)

2. **Effect driven by males and older workers**
   - Males: -0.43 leftward shift (p = 0.013*)
   - Females: -0.01 (no effect)
   - Old: -0.46 leftward shift (p = 0.065†)
   - Young: -0.14 (no effect)

3. **Political shift partially mediated by job insecurity**
   - But substantial direct effect remains
   - Insecurity is mechanism but not sole driver

4. **Effect is ideological, not policy-specific**
   - Primary dimension: left-right placement
   - Secondary dimensions: minor and non-significant
   - Suggests shift toward general "state protection" ideology

### Interpretation

**AI-driven occupational disruption triggers political realignment among workers, manifested as demand for state intervention and skepticism of market forces. The effect is strongest among workers with most to lose (older men, primary breadwinners) and is interpreted as a response to perceived institutional failure of markets to protect workers.**

The gender dimension reveals how **economic threat doesn't automatically translate to political mobilization** — social position, political efficacy, and labor market integration all mediate the translation from economic anxiety to political action.
